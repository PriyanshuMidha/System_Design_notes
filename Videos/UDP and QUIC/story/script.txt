== s1 The night of the final
l1 | N | hook | Packet City, eight in the evening. The Grand Cup final starts in one hour, and eight million fans will watch it on the city's streaming app. But on the evening trains, the stream freezes every time a phone changes network. And one lost packet stalls the whole page. Mira has one hour to fix it.
l2 | MIRA | normal | I am Mira, the network engineer on call. Kit, my rookie, come with me. We are going to meet the transports.
l3 | KIT | question | Transports? I only know the app is slow. And what is a packet?
l4 | N | normal | Kit needs the words first. A packet, or datagram, is one small chunk of data sent by itself, like a forty byte D N S question. A port is a number that picks which program gets the data. D N S uses port fifty three. A handshake is the setup messages before real data moves, like TCP's SYN, SYN ACK, ACK. A round trip is the time for a message to go and a reply to come back, fifty milliseconds on a phone network. A retransmission is sending a lost piece again.
l5 | N | normal | Head of line blocking means one missing piece makes everything behind it wait. A stream is one ordered, reliable flow of bytes inside a connection, like the H T M L download in a page load. And T L S is the encryption layer, the lock icon in a browser.
l6 | MIRA | normal | Here is the whole idea. Ask a friend a one word question across a noisy room. Shaking hands first would be silly, you just shout. If they do not hear, you shout again. That is U D P. But if you send a whole book, you want page numbers, and resend page seven, without stopping the other chapters. That is QUIC. Spelled Q U I C, and said, quick.
l7 | N | normal | Picture the stack. The application sits on top. It can use U D P directly, like D N S and games. Or it can use QUIC, which adds streams, retries and T L S encryption, and itself rides on U D P. Under both sits I P, which delivers to an address. In short, U D P is send and hope. QUIC is reliable, encrypted, independent streams built on top of U D P.
== s2 Why they exist
l8 | N | normal | Why do these exist? Every guarantee TCP gives costs a round trip, or bookkeeping somewhere. A three way handshake has to finish before any data moves. In order delivery means one lost packet stalls everything behind it. And congestion control is tuned for a general byte stream, not for one small message.
l9 | TOM | normal | I am Tom, registered mail. Every parcel I carry gets a number, a signature and a receipt. Safe, yes. But I always shake hands first.
l10 | UDI | punch | I am Udi, the postcard man. Address, message, mailbox, gone. No handshake. Ever.
l11 | N | normal | Take two jobs. First, a D N S query. One small request, one small reply. Paying for a handshake to move forty bytes would double the cost of every lookup, in a protocol that runs millions of times a second. Over TCP, two round trips before the answer arrives. Over U D P, one round trip, no setup cost at all.
l12 | N | normal | Second job, a live video frame, or a game position update. It is time sensitive, and loss tolerant. A frame that arrives late is worse than a frame that never arrives, because a newer frame already made it obsolete. Retransmitting a stale packet is actively the wrong behavior.
l13 | UDI | normal | So that is U D P, R F C seven sixty eight, one of the oldest R F Cs still in daily use. It is what is left of I P once you add only port numbers and an optional checksum. One datagram, fire and forget, no connection state anywhere. An app that wants reliability builds exactly the amount it needs. D N S mostly just retries at the resolver, and falls back to TCP only for large or sensitive responses.
l14 | QUINN | normal | And I am Quinn. I exist for a different reason. H T T P two put many streams on one TCP connection. But a single lost I P packet stalls all of them, because TCP has one ordered byte stream. It cannot give stream two's bytes to the app while it waits on stream one's gap. I am QUIC, R F C nine thousand, standardized in twenty twenty one, and I carry H T T P three. I move multiplexing below the reliability layer, over U D P.
l15 | MIRA | punch | That is tonight's crime. The fans' stream is H T T P two over TCP. One lost packet on a train, and the video, the chat and the scoreboard all freeze together. And remember, Kit. U D P is not broken TCP. It is TCP with every optional guarantee removed, so an app that does not need them does not pay for them.
== s3 The mental model
l16 | N | normal | The mental model. U D P is a postcard. You write an address and a message, drop it in the mailbox, and have no idea if it arrived, arrived twice, or arrived before an earlier postcard. There is no U D P connection to speak of. Net dial U D P, in Go, just remembers a destination address locally. It sends nothing to set that up.
l17 | N | normal | QUIC is what happens if postcards are not enough, but you refuse TCP's single registered mail envelope. Instead you put several independent registered mail threads inside one envelope, each with its own sequence numbers, and its own retransmission. A lost page in thread two only reprints thread two, not the whole letter.
l18 | MIRA | normal | Where the analogy breaks down. Real registered mail cannot change delivery address in transit and still be the same letter. QUIC can. A phone switching from Wi-Fi to cellular keeps its envelope identity, its Connection I D, even though its return address changed. It is closer to a courier recognizing your voice on a new phone number than to anything physical mail can do.
== s4 UDP: a datagram, nothing more
l19 | N | normal | First, U D P. A sender just puts an address and a payload on the wire. There is no setup conversation, and the receiver has no built in way to say got it, or I am ready. Every guarantee the app wants, noticing a missing reply, retrying, removing a packet that showed up twice, reordering, is the application's own job.
l20 | UDI | normal | The client sends a datagram. If a reply arrives, lovely. If the network drops it, the client has no idea. Only a timeout tells it to retry. And there is no accept on my side. Every datagram carries the sender's address, so I can reply to any address at any time.
l21 | N | normal | Who actually uses it, and what each one trades away. D N S trades guaranteed delivery. A lost query times out and retries, and a response over five twelve bytes, without E D N S zero, falls back to TCP. Video and voice calls, R T P and Web R T C, trade retransmission. A late frame is discarded, and congestion response is the application's adaptive bitrate. Online games trade ordering of old data. An old position is wrong by the time it lands. And M D N S and S S D P discovery trade a connection at all, because broadcast and multicast only work without one.
l22 | MIRA | warn | Common mistake, Kit. Thinking a U D P connection means the other side is reachable. Net dial U D P sends nothing on the wire. The first sign that something is wrong is a timeout, not a connection error. U D P guarantees exactly one thing, a datagram with a checksum, addressed to a port. Choosing it is a deliberate bet, never an accident of not bothering with TCP.
== s5 QUIC: reliable streams over UDP
l23 | QUINN | normal | Now me. I open one connection over U D P. Then the app opens many independent streams inside it. Each stream is its own mini ordered and reliable pipe, with its own sequence numbers and its own retransmission. Losing a packet on stream one never blocks stream two.
l24 | N | normal | Picture a page load. One stream for the H T M L, one for the main stylesheet, one for a script. The packet carrying part of the stylesheet is lost. The script's stream keeps delivering bytes the instant they arrive. It never waits on the stylesheet's retransmit. Each U D P datagram can carry frames from several streams, and the whole connection is identified by a Connection I D, not by I P and port.
l25 | MIRA | punch | That is the entire reason H T T P three exists. H T T P two already multiplexed, but on top of TCP's one ordered byte stream. So the fix had to move below TCP, onto U D P.
== s6 QUIC's handshake: TLS inside
l26 | N | normal | Now the handshake. TCP plus T L S does a transport handshake first, and a security handshake second. QUIC carries the T L S one point three messages inside its own first packets. The client sends an Initial packet with the Client Hello, and QUIC transport parameters. The server answers with Initial and Handshake packets, Server Hello, certificate, Finished. The client sends its own Finished. After one round trip, the connection is usable for application data.
l27 | TESS | normal | I am Tess, the passport inspector. With TCP plus T L S one point three, two separate round trips happen before the first encrypted byte moves. One for the TCP connection, one for my check. With QUIC, one. And there is no unencrypted QUIC in practice. The spec has no cleartext mode for application data.
l28 | MIRA | normal | Halving the handshake matters most on mobile networks, where one round trip can be fifty to a hundred fifty milliseconds, paid on every cold connection. And note, QUIC is not skipping a security step. The transport and T L S handshakes were designed together.
== s7 0-RTT and the replay risk
l29 | N | normal | Zero R T T resumption. If the client has a session ticket from a previous connection to the same server, it can send real application data in its very first flight, before any round trip completes. Like a regular customer with a loyalty card. They hand it over and start business immediately.
l30 | MAL | warn | Nice first flight. I will just keep a copy. And send it again, later.
l31 | QUINN | warn | An on path attacker captured the first flight, and replays the exact same encrypted packet. The server decrypts it and can act on it, and cannot yet tell the replay from the original.
l32 | N | normal | So zero R T T data must be limited to operations that are safe to receive more than once, like an idempotent G E T for a product page. Never a payment submission.
l33 | MIRA | warn | Common mistake. Treating zero R T T as a faster handshake with no downside. Attached to a charge this card action, it is a real vulnerability, not a theoretical one. The speed gain is real, but the application layer, not QUIC itself, has to respect the trade off.
== s8 Connection migration on the train
l34 | N | normal | Now the evening train. TCP identifies a connection by its four tuple. Source I P, source port, destination I P, destination port. Change the I P, a phone switching from Wi-Fi to cellular, and the four tuple matches no open socket. The connection dies. QUIC identifies a connection by a Connection I D that the endpoints chose themselves, independent of I P and port.
l35 | N | normal | Step by step. One, the phone's I P changes, but its Connection I D, call it C I D seven, does not. Two, the next packet carries C I D seven from a new source address. Three, the server sees a known Connection I D from an address it has not seen. Four, before trusting the new path, it runs path validation, a small challenge and response, so an attacker spoofing the address cannot hijack the connection. Five, the phone answers correctly. Six, the server resumes on the new path, and the application never saw a drop.
l36 | KIT | question | And what happens if the path validation fails?
l37 | MIRA | normal | The server treats it like any unrecognized packet. The migration is rejected, and the connection behaves as if it had to start over. The same outcome TCP would have had. TCP structurally cannot offer migration, because its connection identity is the four tuple.
== s9 Worked example and the options
l38 | N | normal | The whiteboard version. One client, one connection, two streams. The client opens a QUIC connection, a one round trip handshake with T L S inside. Stream one carries a large file. Stream two carries a small request. Stream one loses a packet and retransmits. Stream two is delivered immediately, and the app reads it. Stream one is delivered eventually.
l39 | N | normal | Now trace it. One, the client sends a U D P datagram with a small query, no setup step. Two, for QUIC, it completes the one round trip handshake. Three, it opens stream A and writes sixty four kilobytes. Four, it opens stream B, independently, and writes a small, independent stream, twenty five bytes. Five, the server echoes each stream on its own sequence space. Six, both replies are verified independently.
l40 | N | normal | Measured on this machine, Go one point twenty four, quic go version zero point forty eight point two, over loopback, with the race detector. Both the sixty four kilobyte stream and the twenty five byte stream completed correctly inside five seconds. The large stream never blocked the small one.
l41 | UDI | normal | Compare the options. Raw U D P, that is me. One send, one maybe receive, no state. Use it when you build exactly the reliability you need. The cost, every guarantee is your own code.
l42 | TOM | normal | Me, TCP. One ordered, reliable byte stream, managed by the O S. Use me when simplicity matters more than per stream independence. The cost, head of line blocking across anything multiplexed on top.
l43 | QUINN | normal | Me, QUIC. Many reliable ordered streams over U D P, with T L S one point three built in. Use me for modern web transport, H T T P three, anything wanting independent streams and migration. The cost, more CPU per byte, since I live in userspace, and some middleboxes still block U D P.
l44 | N | normal | And a fourth option, R T P and Web R T C over U D P. The application manages loss tolerance, jitter buffers, and adaptive bitrate. Great for real time audio and video, where late data is worse than lost data. The cost, no delivery guarantee at all, it is entirely the application's problem.
== s10 Trade-offs, failures and monitoring
l45 | N | warn | The trade offs. QUIC moves TCP's work out of the kernel, where one shared, hardware offload friendly implementation lives, into every application's userspace process. That costs more CPU per byte transferred, a real operational cost at very high throughput. Zero R T T replay means idempotent requests only. And plain U D P is the wrong choice the moment delivered, in order, at least once starts to matter even a little. At that point you are reimplementing a worse version of QUIC or TCP.
l46 | REX | warn | I am Rex, the gate guard. And tonight my stadium gate blocks U D P. Some corporate and I S P middleboxes rate limit, deprioritize, or outright block it. It was abused for amplification D Dos attacks.
l47 | TOM | normal | Which is where I come in. When Rex blocks Quinn, the H T T P three client retries over H T T P two on me, automatically. This is why QUIC being the default still needs a working TCP fallback path.
l48 | N | normal | Real systems. H T T P three is served by Google, Cloudflare, Facebook and Meta, and most major C D Ns, as the default for supporting browsers. D N S runs on U D P port fifty three by default, with TCP fallback. Web R T C video calls and most multiplayer game networking run their own protocols over U D P.
l49 | N | normal | Failure modes and fixes. One, U D P blocked or rate limited. Clients detect it, and fall back to TCP. Two, zero R T T replay of a non idempotent request. Restrict zero R T T to idempotent request types at the application layer, not just the transport. Three, migration path validation fails, because a firewall does not let the new path's probe through. The connection falls back to being a new connection, same as TCP would have done.
l50 | MIRA | normal | And what we watch, Kit, on the control wall. U D P packet loss and retransmit rate per stream. Zero R T T accept and reject counts. Connection migration events and their success rate. And the fallback rate from H T T P three to H T T P two. A rising fallback rate usually means a network path is newly blocking U D P.
== s11 Deep dive: what makes QUIC tick
l51 | N | normal | Deep dive, one. Why QUIC cannot just be TCP, but faster. TCP's state machine, congestion control and retransmission all live in kernel code, which changes on operating system release timescales. QUIC lives in userspace so it can evolve as fast as application code ships. A new congestion controller, or a bug fix, goes out with the next binary, not the next kernel update across billions of devices.
l52 | QUINN | normal | Two, packet numbers. TCP sequence numbers describe bytes in the stream, so a retransmission reuses the same sequence number. Then an ACK cannot tell the sender whether it covers the original or the retransmit. That is the retransmission ambiguity problem, and it corrupts R T T estimates. I give every packet, even every retransmission, a strictly increasing number. Packet forty two is lost, the retransmit is packet fifty eight, same data. An ACK for fifty eight is unambiguous.
l53 | N | normal | That ambiguity is why TCP needed Karn's algorithm, to avoid being fooled by the ACK of a retransmitted segment. QUIC removes the ambiguity at the packet numbering level, instead of patching around it.
l54 | N | normal | Three, loss recovery and congestion control are reimplemented inside QUIC, using these packet number based ACKs and a pluggable congestion controller. Most implementations ship a CUBIC or BBR like algorithm. The same ideas as the TCP note, but running in userspace per connection, instead of in the kernel per socket.
l55 | N | normal | Four, multipath. Because a connection is identified by a Connection I D, and path validation is already in the spec for migration, QUIC is a more natural base than TCP for using several paths at once, Wi-Fi and cellular together, not just moving between them. That is an active area of extension work, not yet universal.
l56 | N | normal | Five, why D N S mostly stays on U D P. D N S over QUIC, called D o Q, R F C nine thousand two hundred fifty, exists, and gives privacy and fast resumption. But a typical query is already one datagram each way, so there is little head of line blocking to fix. QUIC earns its keep when many exchanges share one connection. That is H T T P's situation, not a single lookup's.
== s12 In Go: the code
g1 | N | normal | Now the code, in Go. Two halves. A plain U D P echo that goes silent on loss. And a QUIC connection carrying two independent streams over loopback. The U D P half is standard library only. The QUIC half uses the quic go module. Run it with go vet and go test dash race.
g2 | N | normal | First, echo U D P. A buffer of fifteen hundred bytes, an Ethernet safe size. Read from U D P blocks until a datagram arrives, and returns both the payload and the sender's address. There is no accept. Write to U D P replies directly. If that reply is lost, the original sender gets no signal.
g3 | N | normal | Next, send and wait. Dial U D P with a remote address sends nothing on the wire. It only remembers the address. Write sends the message. Set read deadline is what creates the timeout. Without it, read would block forever. And the error after the deadline is the only signal. It looks the same as, the server does not exist.
g4 | N | normal | QUIC needs T L S. The self signed T L S config function generates a two thousand forty eight bit R S A key and a certificate, in memory. Next protos sets the A L P N identifier, the same mechanism that negotiates h three. No cleartext QUIC.
g5 | N | normal | The server. Accept completes the handshake and returns one connection. Accept stream waits for each new stream, and every stream is handed to its own goroutine running io copy. A slow stream never stalls another. One shared loop would bring head of line blocking back, just inside application code.
g6 | N | normal | The client. Open stream sync opens an independent stream. Write the message, then stream close half closes the write side, so the server sees end of file. Read full reads back exactly the same length. Two goroutines calling this get two genuinely separate streams.
g7 | N | normal | And the wiring. Listen binds loopback, port zero, so the O S picks a free port, with a ten second idle timeout. Dial skips verification only because the demo certificate has no C A. Real clients must verify the chain. The test then proves it, a sixty four kilobyte stream never blocks the twenty five byte one.
== s13 Interview round
q0 | N | hook | Interview round. Kit asks, Mira answers.
q1 | KIT | question | What exactly does U D P guarantee, and what does it not?
q2 | MIRA | normal | Almost nothing beyond, here is a datagram with a checksum, addressed to this port. No setup, no ordering, no retransmission, and no notice to the sender when one is lost. The application must build any of that.
q3 | KIT | question | How does QUIC fix head of line blocking without giving up reliability?
q4 | MIRA | normal | By multiplexing streams below the reliability layer. Each stream has its own sequence space and retransmission, so a lost packet only stalls its own stream. TCP has one byte stream, so H T T P two's streams all wait on the same gap.
q5 | KIT | question | Why is the QUIC handshake one round trip, when TCP plus T L S one point three needs two?
q6 | MIRA | normal | Because the transport handshake and the T L S handshake are the same handshake. The T L S messages ride inside QUIC's own first packets, instead of waiting for a separate TCP handshake to finish.
q7 | KIT | question | The trap. Is QUIC unreliable, because it runs over U D P?
q8 | MIRA | normal | No. QUIC rebuilds reliability, per stream ordering and congestion control on top of U D P. The datagram transport underneath is unreliable. The layer the application talks to is not.
q9 | KIT | question | What is zero R T T data, and what is its risk?
q10 | MIRA | normal | Application data in the very first flight, using a session ticket. The risk is replay, so restrict it to operations that are safe to execute more than once.
q11 | KIT | question | How does QUIC survive an I P change mid connection?
q12 | MIRA | normal | The connection is identified by a Connection I D, not the four tuple. A packet from a new address with a known I D triggers path validation, and then the connection continues on the new path.
q13 | KIT | question | Why does every packet, even a retransmission, get a new packet number?
q14 | MIRA | normal | So an ACK is unambiguous about which transmission it covers. Reusing an identifier makes the ACK ambiguous and corrupts R T T measurement.
