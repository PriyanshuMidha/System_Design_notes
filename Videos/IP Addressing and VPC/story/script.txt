== s1 Level 1: one public address, many devices
l1 | N | hook | Your home has a laptop, two phones, and a television. But your internet provider gives you exactly one public address. Each device still needs its own address, so replies come back to the right one. And at work, a cloud team must carve one big address range into pieces, for web servers, and for databases. This video explains how addresses are numbered, split up, and shared.
l2 | N | normal | Level one, the words. An I P address is a number that names one device on a network, like ten dot zero dot one dot seventeen. A bit is one binary digit, zero or one. An I P version four address is thirty two of them. The prefix, slash twenty four, means the first twenty four bits are fixed.
l3 | N | normal | The network part is the shared street, and the host part is the house number. In ten dot zero dot one, dot seventeen, the street is ten dot zero dot one, and the house is seventeen.
l4 | N | normal | A port names one program or connection on a device, like four forty three for H T T P S. A router forwards packets toward their destination. A public address is reachable from the internet, like eight dot eight dot eight dot eight. A private one only works inside your network, like one ninety two dot one sixty eight dot one dot five. And a route table is a list of rules, traffic for this range goes there.

== s2 Level 1: why addressing and NAT exist
l7 | N | normal | Why all this? I P version four addresses are thirty two bit numbers, so there can only ever be two to the thirty two of them. About four point three billion. By the nineteen nineties it was clear the internet would run out, if every device got a globally unique one. Today there are more phones than that on the planet.
l8 | N | normal | Two fixes shipped. First, C I D R, classless inter domain routing, replaced the old rigid class A, B and C system with flexible prefix lengths, so allocators could hand out exactly the size an organisation needed.
l9 | N | normal | Second, private addressing, plus N A T. Every home and company reuses the same few private ranges internally, and only one N A T box's public address is visible to the outside world.
l10 | N | normal | Think of it with numbers. Your home, your office, and a thousand other unrelated networks can all use one ninety two dot one sixty eight dot one dot zero slash twenty four internally, without any conflict, because none of those addresses ever appear on the public internet.
l11 | N | punch | So C I D R solved allocation waste. N A T solved scarcity, by letting many devices share one public address.

l15 | NORA | normal | And I am Nora, the front desk. The building has one public street address, but many internal apartments. Mail for the front desk gets routed to the right apartment, because I keep a log of who is expecting a delivery. That log is the translation table, keyed by port.
l16 | N | normal | Where the analogy bends. A real front desk forwards mail that was expected. N A T also handles brand new outbound mail from any apartment, handing it a tracking number, a port, on the fly, so the reply knows the way back.
== s4 Level 2: CIDR and prefix math
l17 | N | normal | Level two. The core of it, prefix math. Each extra bit of prefix halves a block. Each fewer bit doubles it. A slash eight is sixteen million seven hundred seventy seven thousand two hundred sixteen addresses, a whole legacy class A.
l18 | N | normal | Slash sixteen is sixty five thousand five hundred thirty six addresses, usually a whole V P C. Slash twenty is four thousand ninety six, one subnet per availability zone. Slash twenty four is two hundred fifty six, with two hundred fifty four usable, a typical L A N. Slash thirty is four addresses, two usable, a router to router link. Slash thirty one is two addresses, both usable, by R F C thirty twenty one. And slash thirty two is one address, a single host route.
l19 | N | normal | Now split a block. You are handed ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash sixteen for a V P C. You need subnets of slash twenty, four thousand ninety six addresses each. The difference is four bits, so two to the four, sixteen, equal slash twenties fit inside the slash sixteen.
l20 | CID | normal | The first starts at ten dot zero dot zero dot zero, through ten dot zero dot fifteen dot two fifty five. The next starts at ten dot zero dot sixteen, then ten dot zero dot thirty two, then ten dot zero dot forty eight. Each step adds four thousand ninety six addresses.
l21 | N | warn | The mistake to avoid. A bigger prefix number is not a bigger block. It is the opposite. Slash twenty four is smaller than slash sixteen, because a longer prefix leaves fewer bits for the hosts. Smaller slash N number, bigger block.
== s5 Level 2: IPv4 versus IPv6
l22 | N | normal | I P version four's shortage is exactly why I P version six exists. A one hundred twenty eight bit address space, about three hundred forty undecillion addresses, written as eight colon separated groups of hexadecimal digits, and shortened by dropping leading zeros.
l23 | N | normal | Compare. Version four, thirty two bits, about four point three billion addresses, needs N A T to share them, written as four dotted bytes. Version six, one hundred twenty eight bits, every device can have a globally routable address, written as eight hex groups. And the private equivalent of R F C eighteen eighteen is unique local addresses, f c zero zero slash seven, for the same organisational reasons.
l24 | N | normal | Most hosts today are dual stack. They hold both an address of each kind, and prefer version six when both sides support it. That is what the quad A record in D N S is for. Abundance removes the scarcity that made N A T necessary, but private ranges still exist.
== s6 Level 2: private ranges
l25 | N | normal | Three address blocks are reserved by R F C eighteen eighteen, never to appear on the public internet. So anyone can reuse them internally, without a real conflict.
l26 | N | normal | Ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash eight, about sixteen point seven million addresses, for large company networks, and most cloud V P Cs. One seventy two dot sixteen slash twelve, about one million, Docker's default bridge range. And one ninety two dot one sixty eight slash sixteen, sixty five thousand, home routers.
l27 | N | normal | Other special ranges. One twenty seven slash eight is loopback, meaning, this machine. And one sixty nine dot two fifty four slash sixteen is link local. If you see one of the three private ranges in a log or config, it is an instant signal, this is internal. If you see one outside a private network, something is misconfigured.
== s7 Level 2: NAT and PAT
l28 | N | normal | Now N A T, network address translation. It rewrites the source address of outbound packets, from a private address to a public one, and reverses the rewrite on the way back. Plain one to one N A T saves no addresses by itself. It just maps one private address to one public address.
l29 | N | normal | What lets a whole household share one public address is P A T, port address translation, also called N A T overload. It also rewrites the source port, and uses the pair, public address and public port, as the key to send each reply back to the right private device.
l30 | N | normal | A trace with real numbers. Dev, at one ninety two dot one sixty eight dot one dot forty two, port fifty one thousand, opens a connection to example dot com, port four forty three.
l31 | NORA | normal | Noted. I add a table entry. One ninety two dot one sixty eight dot one dot forty two, port fifty one thousand, maps to two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot nine, port forty thousand one. I rewrite the source, and send it out.
l32 | N | normal | The server replies to two oh three dot zero dot one thirteen dot nine, port forty thousand one. The router looks up forty thousand one in its table, finds Dev's address and port, rewrites the destination back, and delivers it.
l33 | N | normal | Because the key includes the port, one public address can multiplex up to roughly sixty four thousand simultaneous outbound connections, bounded by the sixteen bit port space, minus what the router reserves.
l34 | N | warn | A common mistake. N A T and a firewall are not the same thing. N A T incidentally blocks unsolicited inbound traffic, because no table entry exists for it to match. But its real job is address and port translation, not security policy.
== s8 Level 2: AWS VPC
l35 | N | normal | Now the cloud. A V P C, virtual private cloud, is your own private address space inside A W S, sliced into subnets, each pinned to one availability zone. Each subnet has a route table, deciding where traffic for each destination prefix goes.
l36 | VIC | normal | I am Vic, a cloud architect. And here is the surprise. There is no separate public subnet resource in A W S. Public or private is entirely about the route table.
l37 | N | normal | A public subnet's route table sends zero dot zero dot zero dot zero slash zero, everything not matched more specifically, to an internet gateway. And its instances need a public or elastic address to be reachable.
l38 | N | normal | A private subnet's route table sends that default route to a N A T gateway, sitting in a public subnet. Instances there have only private addresses. They can reach out, to pull packages, or call external A P Is, but nothing from the internet can start a connection to them.
l39 | N | normal | The N A T gateway holds one elastic address, and does P A T exactly like a home router, only managed and scaled by A W S. And there is a third tier, isolated subnets, for databases. They have no default route at all, not even outbound, so a compromised database host cannot send data out to the internet.
== s9 Level 2: five reserved addresses
l40 | N | normal | One more detail. A plain slash twenty four reserves two addresses, leaving two hundred fifty four. A W S reserves five. In ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash twenty four, dot zero is the network address. Dot one is the V P C router. Dot two is the A W S D N S resolver. Dot three is reserved for future use. And dot two fifty five is the broadcast address, reserved even though A W S does not support broadcast.
l41 | RAE | normal | I am Rae, the V P C router. I live at dot one in every subnet.
l42 | N | normal | So a slash twenty four in A W S has two hundred fifty one usable addresses, not two hundred fifty four. Sizing a subnet for exactly the number of instances you need is a common off by a few error. Always size for a small buffer above the raw count.
== s10 Level 2: a worked example, splitting a block
l43 | N | normal | The heart of all C I D R math. Convert an address to a thirty two bit integer, add a step size, and convert back. Splitting ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash sixteen into slash twenties.
l44 | N | normal | Step one. The step is one shifted left by thirty two minus twenty, that is one shifted left by twelve, four thousand ninety six. Each slash twenty spans four thousand ninety six addresses.
l45 | N | normal | Step two. Subnet zero starts at ten dot zero dot zero dot zero. Subnet one starts four thousand ninety six addresses later, at ten dot zero dot sixteen dot zero. Subnet two at ten dot zero dot thirty two dot zero. And so on, until sixteen subnets are produced. Four thousand ninety six is a power of two, so every step lands exactly on a boundary.
== s11 Level 3: In Go, subnet math
g1 | N | normal | Now the code, in Go. Three small functions. How many usable hosts in a prefix. Where a block begins and ends. And splitting a block into equal subnets.
g2 | N | normal | Usable hosts takes the prefix length. It rejects anything outside zero to thirty two. A slash thirty two is one host. A slash thirty one is two, because of the point to point exception. Every other prefix is one shifted left by thirty two minus the prefix, minus two, for the network and broadcast addresses.
g3 | N | normal | Subnets takes a parent block and a new, longer prefix. The count is one shifted left by the difference in prefixes, that is, two to the four, sixteen, in our example. The step is one shifted left by thirty two minus the new prefix.
g4 | N | normal | A loop then produces each subnet, by adding index times step to the parent's base, and formatting it as a block. Two tiny helpers convert between an address and a plain integer, so that adding a step is ordinary integer addition, with no byte by byte carry logic to get wrong.
g5 | N | normal | The tests check usable hosts for the common prefixes, the sixteen subnets of the worked example, and whether an address is in a private range. All pass with the race detector on.
== s12 Level 3: variants and comparison
l46 | N | normal | A few approaches side by side. Static V L S M subnetting, picking a prefix per subnet by its expected size, for stable needs, but resizing later is disruptive. D H C P leases addresses from a subnet's range, for networks where hosts come and go, but it does not change the subnet's size.
l47 | N | normal | N A T and P A T for I P version four, which breaks true end to end addressing, and needs connection tracking state. I P version six with no N A T, for new networks, but it needs dual stack support during a long transition. And V P C peering, or a transit gateway, to route between V P Cs without N A T, where the address ranges must not overlap.
== s13 Level 3: trade-offs, failures and what to watch
t1 | N | normal | The trade offs. N A T breaks the one address per host assumption, which some protocols need. Incoming connections, voice over I P, peer to peer, so you need workarounds like stun and turn, or hairpin N A T.
t2 | N | normal | Over sized subnets waste space. A slash sixteen for ten hosts wastes over sixty five thousand addresses. Over small ones force a painful renumbering. Plan for projected growth, and remember the five reserved addresses. A N A T gateway costs money per hour and per gigabyte, so for air gapped workloads an isolated subnet is cheaper and safer. And overlapping ranges between V P Cs you will eventually peer, for example after an acquisition, are expensive to fix. Choose non overlapping ranges company wide, up front.
t3 | N | warn | Four production failures. Overlapping ranges when peering two V P Cs. Renumber one side, or use a transit gateway with careful routes. There is no way to route between identical ranges. A single N A T gateway as a bottleneck. Use one per availability zone, for resilience, and to avoid cross zone transfer charges.
t4 | N | warn | Running out of addresses in a subnet sized too small for autoscaling. Size for peak scale out, not the average. And a private subnet with no N A T route, so instances cannot pull container images or call external A P Is. Check that the default route really points at a N A T gateway.
t5 | N | normal | What to monitor. N A T gateway bandwidth and connection count, because each gateway has throughput limits. Free addresses remaining per subnet. And unexpected cross zone or cross region data transfer costs, from misrouted traffic.
== s14 Level 3: deep dive
d1 | N | normal | A few deeper points. First, why P A T's limit is about sixty four thousand. The table's public side key is public address plus public port, and ports are sixteen bits, so one address can track at most sixty five thousand five hundred thirty six simultaneous connections to distinct destinations. In practice routers reserve some, so real limits are tighter. Carrier grade N A T gives each subscriber a range of ports on a shared public address, instead of the whole space.
d2 | N | normal | Second, C I D R versus classful. Before nineteen ninety three, only class A, slash eight, class B, slash sixteen, and class C, slash twenty four, existed. If you needed three hundred addresses, you got a wasteful slash sixteen. C I D R handed out a slash twenty three instead, five hundred ten usable. It also shrank routing tables, through route aggregation, where an I S P advertises one summary prefix for many customer blocks.
d3 | N | normal | Third, V P C route precedence. A W S route tables use longest prefix match, like internet routers. A route for ten dot zero dot one dot zero slash twenty four beats ten dot zero slash sixteen, for a destination inside the smaller block, regardless of the order the routes were added. That is how a V P C sends most traffic to a N A T gateway, but sends a peered V P C's range directly across the peering connection.
d4 | N | normal | Fourth, dual stack realities. Most clouds default new V P Cs to dual stack, but a huge share of enterprise tooling still assumes version four only. So N A T and private range planning remain a day one decision, even now.
d5 | N | normal | And last, the isolated tier most tutorials skip. Databases and internal caches often sit in a subnet with no default route at all, reachable only from the application tier's security group. A layer beyond public versus private, which sharply reduces the blast radius of a compromise.
== s15 Interview round
q0 | N | hook | Interview round. Seven questions you will really get.
q1 | N | normal | One. What does slash twenty four mean, and how many usable hosts? The first twenty four of thirty two bits are the network prefix, leaving eight bits, two hundred fifty six addresses. Two are reserved, so two hundred fifty four usable. In an A W S subnet, five are reserved, leaving two hundred fifty one.
q2 | N | normal | Two. A trap. N A T and a firewall are the same thing. No. N A T incidentally blocks unsolicited inbound traffic, but its job is address translation and connection tracking. A firewall allows or denies traffic by policy.
q3 | N | normal | Three. How does P A T let many devices share one address? It rewrites both source address and source port, and keeps a table from public address and port back to the private address and port. Because the key includes the port, one address multiplexes tens of thousands of connections.
q4 | N | normal | Four. What makes an A W S subnet public or private? Only its route table. Default route to an internet gateway, with public or elastic addresses, is public. Default route to a N A T gateway, outbound only, is private. No default route at all is fully isolated.
q5 | N | normal | Five. Why not give every private subnet ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash twenty four? Inside one V P C in isolation it is fine. But peering with another V P C, or on premises, that uses the same range makes routing ambiguous. A packet for ten dot zero dot zero dot five could belong to either.
q6 | N | normal | Six. Why does I P version six not need N A T? Its one hundred twenty eight bit space gives every device a globally unique routable address, removing the scarcity. Unique local ranges still exist, and N A T is optional there, not structural.
q7 | N | normal | Seven. Exactly which five addresses does A W S reserve, and why five? In ten dot zero dot zero dot zero slash twenty four. Dot zero, the network. Dot one, the V P C router. Dot two, the D N S resolver. Dot three, reserved for future use. And dot two fifty five, the broadcast equivalent. A plain slash twenty four reserves only the first and last. A W S adds three.
